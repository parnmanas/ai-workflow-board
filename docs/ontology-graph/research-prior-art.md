# Prior-Art Autopsy: Code-Understanding Products — What They Built, and What Actually Killed or Saved Them (for AWB Ontology Graph)

Date: 2026-08-22. Target: this document does not add new technology decisions on top of `research-extraction.md`, `research-ontology.md`, `research-storage.md`, `research-visualization.md`, or `research-incremental.md` — it stress-tests all four with the one question none of them asked: *has anyone actually shipped something like this before, and if so, what specifically made it survive or die?* Six prior-art subjects, chosen because each is either the closest commercial analog (Sourcegraph, CodeSee), the closest cautionary tale (Sourcetrail), the closest cheap-and-proven baseline (Aider), or the closest evidence of what the best-funded 2026 competitors chose *not* to build (Cursor, Devin).

**Bottom line up front:** Two companies attacked almost exactly AWB's problem — an explicit, structural, visual map of "what depends on what" in a codebase, sold as a destination a developer opens to understand their code — and both went to zero within roughly a two-year window despite real usage and real funding: Sourcetrail was archived in December 2021, about two years after Coati Software open-sourced it (https://github.com/CoatiSoftware/Sourcetrail), and CodeSee shut down in February 2024, about two years after its last $7M raise, despite having "more than doubled the number of new product logos in 2023 and grew by many thousands of free users" (Shanea Leven, CodeSee CEO, shutdown announcement, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/shaneak_i-am-very-sad-to-announce-officially-activity-7163970333912289281-DaOb). Three people who separately tried to monetize adjacent products — the maintainer of `code2flow`, the founder of the static-analysis SaaS QuantifiedCode, and a JetBrains-subscription developer — converged, unprompted, on the same diagnosis in the Hacker News thread discussing Sourcetrail's discontinuation (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=28637193): call-graph/structure visualization is used *occasionally*, not daily; JetBrains/VS Code already give most of the value for free via go-to-definition and find-references; and the people who love the free diagrams are not the people who hold a budget for them. **Sourcegraph avoided this trap not by building a better diagram but by never selling "understand your code" as the product** — it sells operational-risk reduction (security-incident blast radius, compliance audits, cross-repo migrations) to buyers whose job title is exactly that, wrapping the same graph-adjacent machinery AWB is building in a completely different value proposition, and its own newest positioning (2026) states this explicitly: it is now sold as infrastructure *for coding agents* doing "1000x more code searches" than humans, not a UI a human opens (https://sourcegraph.com/blog/why-sourcegraph-and-amp-are-becoming-independent-companies). It took Sourcegraph roughly a decade, a self-hosted-first pivot forced by an enterprise trust barrier, and a reported ~$248M in funding to get there (https://research.contrary.com/company/sourcegraph). **Aider's repo-map is the sharpest validation of the opposite lesson**: the cheapest conceivable version of this idea — tree-sitter extraction, personalized PageRank, a token budget enforced by binary search, rebuilt from scratch on every single invocation with **zero persistent storage** — is proven (by direct inspection of its source, not a blog claim) to measurably shape what an LLM coding agent sees, which is direct evidence that AWB's mandatory Tier 1 (already decided in `research-extraction.md` §5.1) is sufficient on its own to deliver real agent-facing value, and that every tier stacked above it must earn its keep against that free baseline. And **the two fastest-growing AI coding products of 2026, Cursor and Devin, both explicitly chose not to build what AWB is building**: Cursor's runtime answer to "understand this codebase" is embeddings-plus-grep-plus-AST-chunking with no persistent symbol graph at all (https://cursor.com/blog/secure-codebase-indexing), and Devin's DeepWiki treats a graph as a generated artifact *for humans* (a wiki with diagrams) while the agent's actual moment-to-moment tool is agentic multi-hop search, not graph traversal (https://docs.devin.ai/work-with-devin/deepwiki). AWB's bet — a persistent, queryable, explicit ontology graph — is structurally Sourcegraph's bet, not Cursor's or Devin's, which means AWB inherits Sourcegraph's adoption risk without inheriting Sourcegraph's decade of runway or its risk-reduction sales motion. The concrete implication, expanded into eight numbered pitfalls in §6, is that the feature must be consumed the way Sourcegraph's code-intelligence layer is consumed inside a larger workflow — an ingredient other tools and agents reach for mid-task — never the way Sourcetrail or CodeSee tried to sell it: a standalone destination view a person opens to "understand code."

## 1. Sourcegraph — the one that scaled, and specifically why

### 1.1 Precise (SCIP) vs. search-based: which one is the default, and which is the upgrade

`research-extraction.md` §4 point 1 already extracts and adopts Sourcegraph's fallback-layering pattern ("the graph is a union with a `confidence`/`source` property on every edge"). Worth confirming from the primary source directly, because it is easy to mis-read the two tiers as peers: Sourcegraph's own docs state search-based navigation, "powered by tools like ctags and tree-sitter," is available "out-of-the-box" and used as the baseline, while precise navigation via SCIP/LSIF is "precomputed" and must be "periodically generated and uploaded" — precise results are used *whenever available*, and imprecise search-based results silently fill the gaps when a dependency repo lacks an index (https://sourcegraph.com/docs/code-search/code-navigation/precise_code_navigation). This is not a hybrid designed in the abstract; it is the direct, load-bearing consequence of SCIP indexers being whole-project, dependency-requiring, and non-incremental (`research-extraction.md` §2.3, §4 point 6) — Sourcegraph could not have shipped precise-only navigation and still covered every repo a customer connects, so search-based-as-default was forced by the same operational reality AWB's own Tier 1/Tier 2 split responds to. The auto-indexing scheduler that keeps the precise layer fresh runs on a fixed 2-minute sweep interval, batches 2,500 repositories per cycle, and enforces a 24-hour minimum cooldown before the same repository is reconsidered (`PRECISE_CODE_INTEL_AUTO_INDEXING_TASK_INTERVAL`, `_REPOSITORY_BATCH_SIZE`, `_REPOSITORY_PROCESS_DELAY`; https://sourcegraph.com/docs/code-search/code-navigation/auto_indexing) — the same three-knob shape (sweep interval, batch cap, cooldown) `research-incremental.md` §5.2 already recommends for AWB's own Tier-3 LLM-enrichment scheduler, independently arrived at for a different tier of a comparable pipeline.

### 1.2 Why it scaled and stayed alive commercially

Sourcegraph launched in 2013 (founders Quinn Slack and Beyang Liu, who met at Palantir and were separately frustrated by the absence of Google-style internal code search at previous jobs) with a cloud-hosted product first. That failed on a specific, avoidable trust barrier: enterprise developers would not upload proprietary source to an external SaaS. An independent business-history write-up is direct about the consequence, drawing on Sourcegraph's own account of the pivot (https://sourcegraph.com/blog/from-saas-to-on-premises, synthesized in https://research.contrary.com/company/sourcegraph): the company ran **self-hosted-first for eight years**, and only relaunched a cloud offering in September 2022, after landing large self-hosted enterprise deployments (Uber, Plaid) and adding SOC 2 Type II compliance. The lesson generalizes directly to AWB's own deployment model: AWB already runs the ontology graph inside the customer's existing SQLite/Postgres instance (`research-storage.md` §1, §8) rather than as an external service, which is the *outcome* Sourcegraph took years to reach the hard way — AWB should not treat that as free, but should recognize it as the correct default it already has, not a corner it's cutting.

The second, more load-bearing reason Sourcegraph survived where Sourcetrail and CodeSee did not: it never asked a developer to value "understanding code" in the abstract. Sourcegraph's own blog on the build-vs-buy decision quantifies exactly how large the underlying engineering problem is — its code-intelligence layer alone (cross-repository reference resolution, re-indexing scheduling, staleness handling) is "roughly 96,000 lines of Go," and the company's own cost model for a customer building the equivalent in-house ranges from **8–12 engineers over 12–18 months (~$5.5–9.4M) for 500 repos on one host, up to 20–30 engineers over 24–36 months (~$27.3–58.0M) for 5,000+ repos in a regulated environment** — with a 5-person maintenance team costing "$1.1M per year, indefinitely" after that (https://sourcegraph.com/blog/what-it-actually-takes-to-run-code-intelligence-in-house). That is not a story about diagrams; it is a story about a company selling avoidance of a seven-to-eight-figure internal build. AWB is not attempting anything close to that scope (single-workspace, folder-scoped, no multi-repo permission sync, no twelve-code-host integration matrix per `research-storage.md` §7.3's Glean comparison) — the number is cited here specifically as a boundary marker: it is the evidence that "build the general enterprise version of this" is a different, much larger problem than AWB's actual scope, and conflating the two would be a scoping error, not an ambition to strive for.

### 1.3 What the enterprise adoption pattern actually looks like

The clearest primary-source example of Sourcegraph's *land* motion is Nutanix's Log4j (CVE-2021-44228) response: engineer Jon used code search to find every instance of the vulnerable `JMSAppender` pattern across Nutanix's codebase and shipped a fix — "that took almost less than five minutes" for the query itself, with the company confidently remediating and shipping patches within **4 days** overall (https://sourcegraph.com/case-studies/nutanix-fixed-log4j-with-sourcegraph). That is the adoption pattern in one story: the buyer is not "a developer who wants to understand the codebase," it is an organization with an active, dated, board-visible incident, and the tool is judged entirely on time-to-remediation. Qualtrics (1,000+ developers) and Palo Alto Networks (2,000+ developers) are cited as proven multi-repository deployments at the platform-search stage that follows this kind of initial urgent adoption (search synthesis of Sourcegraph's own resources pages, cross-checked against the customer list in Contrary Research's independent write-up: Uber, Lyft, Plaid, Dropbox, Redfin, Reddit, Databricks — https://research.contrary.com/company/sourcegraph). Reported revenue grew from $31M (2024) to $50M (2025), against a reported $2.6B valuation and ~$248M raised across funding rounds including a $125–150M Series D (July 2021) — treat these last figures as third-party financial-data-aggregator estimates for a private company, not audited disclosures, cited here only for order-of-magnitude confidence that the self-hosted-first, incident-driven motion is what actually produced a durable business, not a one-off anecdote.

Most tellingly for AWB's own framing: Sourcegraph's newest public positioning (2026) explicitly reframes its product away from a human destination and toward *agent* infrastructure — "AI is writing more code than humans ever could, and performing 1000x more code searches," describing the code-search layer as "mission-critical AI infrastructure" that coding agents consume, distinct enough from its frontier-agent product (Amp) that the two are becoming separate companies (https://sourcegraph.com/blog/why-sourcegraph-and-amp-are-becoming-independent-companies). Even the incumbent that won by selling to humans first is, in 2026, repositioning its graph-adjacent machinery as something agents call, not something people browse — which is precisely the shape `research-extraction.md` §5.3's MCP tool list (`graph_find_symbol`, `graph_neighbors`, `graph_blast_radius`) already assumes for AWB.

## 2. Sourcetrail — the maintenance-burden-meets-thin-market autopsy

### 2.1 What Coati Software built, and why

Sourcetrail began life as "Coati" in 2016, created by Eberhard Gräther after an experience at Google Chrome where a feature he expected to take one to two hours took an entire month, purely because of how hard the existing codebase was to navigate (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourcetrail). The product — a free, cross-platform (Windows/macOS/Linux) desktop application supporting C, C++, Java, and Python, indexing a codebase into an interactive dependency graph with click-to-navigate "who calls who" / "who implements who" views — was released commercially, then made fully free and open-source under GPLv3 in November 2019 (https://github.com/CoatiSoftware/Sourcetrail). It accumulated **16,000+ GitHub stars by 2021**, a genuinely large number for a developer tool, and drew enough personal loyalty that users supported it directly on Patreon (HN thread comment from `disdi89`: "I loved the software... Even supported on Patreon").

### 2.2 Why it was archived in 2021 — the stated reasons

The company's own "Discontinue Sourcetrail" post (https://sourcetrail.com/blog/discontinue_sourcetrail/, published September 2021; the domain is offline as of this research, corroborated via the Hacker News thread that discussed and linked it at the time, https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=28637193, and via the GitHub repository's own archival record) is consistently summarized across independent secondary sources as citing three compounding factors: (1) the burden of maintaining a cross-platform C++ application with **separate per-language indexers spanning C, C++, Java, and Python** against constantly-evolving compiler/build-system dependencies; (2) both founders taking new full-time jobs plus a relocation that made continued collaboration difficult; and (3) — the business-model fact most directly relevant to AWB — that even after the 2019 open-sourcing, "there were just not enough paid customers to adequately compensate the company for its work," and that "not every developer saw the value of the tool, which made it hard to sell." The GitHub repository was archived by its owner on **December 14, 2021**, with a final release (2021.4.19) on November 30, 2021.

### 2.3 Independent corroboration from the HN community

The most valuable evidence here is not the announcement itself but the reaction to it, because three people who were *not* Sourcetrail's team, building *adjacent* products, converged unprompted on the same diagnosis:

- `scottrogowski`, maintainer of a similar (smaller) open-source call-graph tool (`code2flow`), on why he never turned it into a SaaS: **"call graph visualizers are the sort of thing that you would need only once or only every once in a while. That sort of software is very difficult to monetize... developers might have found it useful occasionally but it's not something most developers would use daily or even monthly - so it's hard to justify a subscription."**
- `ThePhysicist`, founder of QuantifiedCode, a static-analysis-and-automated-refactoring SaaS from the same era, independently reporting the identical failure mode from a different product in the same space: **"developer tools are really hard to monetize, except maybe from the security/compliance/productivity angle (which doesn't really target developers though, more their superiors)."**
- `fxtentacle`, on why the *category* is structurally hard regardless of quality: **"everyone who's willing to pay for a good IDE is already subscribed to JetBrains... new developer tools are difficult to monetize because the established ones are already exceptionally good. There's no customer pain to alleviate."**
- A direct value-proposition exchange between `nyanpasu64` and `jschwartzi` is the sharpest evidence of all: `nyanpasu64` asks whether Sourcetrail is "measurably better than having an IDE with 2 panes, 'go to definition' and 'find all references', and a document for taking notes? In my experience it's not really." `jschwartzi`'s defense is candid about the actual margin: **"Well it's a matter of friction. Clion gives you the same information but for each item in the chain you have to click on it bunches. In Sourcetrail you mouse over the symbol and click once."** A one-click-vs-several-clicks friction reduction is a real but thin margin to build a company on when the free alternative (already installed, already paid for by a JetBrains subscription most professional developers already have) covers 80% of the value.

(All quotes: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=28637193.)

### 2.4 The concrete lesson for AWB

The failure was not technical. Sourcetrail's indexers worked, its UX was good enough to earn Patreon supporters and 16,000+ stars, and its core capability (click a symbol, see who calls it and who it calls) is exactly the capability `research-extraction.md` §5.3's `graph_neighbors`/`graph_call_path` MCP tools also provide. The failure was that the capability was packaged as **a separate destination application** whose entire reason to open was "come here to understand your code" — a job developers do occasionally, against a free, already-installed IDE alternative that does most of the same job with more friction but zero switching cost. AWB does not face Sourcetrail's cross-platform-desktop-app maintenance burden (it is a hosted feature of an existing web platform, and `research-extraction.md` §5.1's tree-sitter-only mandatory tier is a single WASM dependency, not several native per-language indexers) — but it faces the identical *positioning* risk if the ontology graph ships primarily as a "Graph" tab a human opens to browse, rather than as data AWB's agents already reach for mid-task. §6 below turns this into pitfall #1.

## 3. CodeSee — funded, growing, AI-pivoted, and still died

### 3.1 What CodeSee built

CodeSee (founded 2019) built a cloud SaaS around **auto-generated, self-updating, collaborative diagrams of a codebase**, marketed explicitly as "a Google Map for your code" (https://www.codesee.io/codebase-maps), across JavaScript, TypeScript, Python, Java, Rust, .NET/C#, Kotlin, and Go. The product surface diversified over its life into several distinct diagram types: static **Codebase Maps** (structure), **Flow Maps** (execution/data-flow tracing with variants, i.e., which code paths actually run for a given trigger), **Review Maps** (auto-generated and posted directly into GitHub pull requests), and **Service Maps** (cross-repository, distributed-tracing-backed views connecting to Datadog/OpenTelemetry) — a materially wider surface than Sourcetrail ever attempted, and one that (unlike Sourcetrail) was embedded directly into an existing workflow developers already do daily: code review. This is the single point where CodeSee's design was structurally *better* aimed at the "recurring workflow, not curiosity" lesson than Sourcetrail's was — and it still was not enough (§3.2–3.4).

### 3.2 The trajectory: funding, headcount, the AI pivot, the shutdown

CodeSee raised a $3M seed round in September 2021 (co-led by Boldstart Ventures and Uncork Capital, with DCVC, Precursor Ventures, and Salesforce Ventures participating), then an additional $7M in January 2022 (led by Wellington Access Ventures and Plexo Capital) — **$10M total** across the two rounds (https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2022/01/20/2370076/0/en/CodeSee-Announces-7M-in-New-Funding-to-Address-Rising-Demand-for-Code-Visualization-and-Understanding.html). By July 2023 the company had **11 employees**. That month it shipped a generative-AI pivot — natural-language querying ("how does logging work," "how is state management being used") layered on top of the existing map product, built in partnership with Microsoft and OpenAI (https://techcrunch.com/2023/07/19/codesee-adds-generative-ai-to-explore-code-base-with-natural-language-queries/). CEO Shanea Leven's own framing of that pivot is the key sentence for what it did *not* change: **"with the addition of AI, our hypotheses and our mission and our use cases don't actually change, but our insight into how people grasp and understand code actually gets enhanced when you add AI onto it."** A further feature, Function Maps, shipped in November 2023 (https://www.codesee.io/function-maps). Seven months after the AI pivot and three months after Function Maps, on **February 22, 2024**, CodeSee announced it was shutting down as an independent company; GitKraken acquired its technology and team on May 14, 2024 (https://www.gitkraken.com/blog/gitkraken-launches-devex-platform-acquires-codesee).

### 3.3 Shanea Leven's own explanation, verbatim

Leven's shutdown post is unusually direct about separating growth from revenue, which is exactly the distinction this document's thesis depends on (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/shaneak_i-am-very-sad-to-announce-officially-activity-7163970333912289281-DaOb):

> "The company had more than doubled the number of new product logos in 2023 and grew by many thousands of free users." ... "our sales growth was inconsistent, and we needed to make revenue faster and more consistently." ... "those choices did not lead to the consistent revenue we needed for us to continue."

And on the underlying difficulty of the product category itself — not a claim of technical failure, but of an unbounded scope problem:

> Solving code understanding would have required "deeply cover[ing] more complex codebases, more IDEs, more tech stacks, and more programming languages which would have taken time." ... "Gen AI has made this space even more complex, both as a tool to help and a source of ever more code."

That last sentence is worth sitting with: the founder's own retrospective is that generative AI made the code-understanding problem **harder to keep up with**, not easier to solve by bolting AI onto the existing product — direct primary-source evidence against the intuitive assumption that "just add an LLM layer" rescues a struggling code-visualization product.

### 3.4 Was this "beautiful diagram nobody opens twice"? — testing the hypothesis against the evidence

The glib framing is directionally right but not literally what the primary sources show. CodeSee did not die from zero adoption — it died from an adoption/monetization **mismatch**: real, growing usage (doubled logos, thousands of new free users in a single year) that did not convert into revenue predictable enough to sustain an 11-person, VC-funded team. That is a more precise and more useful failure mode for AWB to internalize than "nobody used it": the risk is not that the ontology graph gets zero opens, it is that it gets *plenty* of first-opens and exploratory sessions that never become a recurring, load-bearing part of anyone's actual workflow — the same mechanism the Sourcetrail HN commenters named independently in §2.3 (`scottrogowski`: "useful occasionally but not something most developers would use daily or even monthly"). CodeSee's Review Maps feature is the most instructive data point of all precisely because it *was* embedded in a recurring workflow (every PR) and the company still could not convert that into durable revenue — evidence that "embed it in an existing workflow" (this document's own pitfall #1, below) is necessary but was, for CodeSee, not sufficient on its own; the harder unsolved problem was making the recurring touch-point valuable enough, per-touch, that someone would pay for it rather than tolerate it as a free extra.

### 3.5 What happened after

GitKraken folded CodeSee's "code automation and code health capabilities" — its visualization and workflow-automation technology — into a broader "DevEx platform" launched the same day as the acquisition, spanning desktop, IDE, terminal, and browser surfaces across GitHub/GitLab/Bitbucket/Azure DevOps/Jira/Trello (https://www.gitkraken.com/blog/gitkraken-launches-devex-platform-acquires-codesee) — i.e., the technology survived only by being absorbed into a company whose actual product is a wider developer-workflow bundle, not a code-visualization business on its own. Separately, a small startup, NanoAPI, launched on Hacker News in November 2024 explicitly positioning itself as CodeSee's spiritual successor ("Carrying the Torch: NanoAPI Picks Up Where CodeSee Left Off," https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42114034) — but notably narrowed the value proposition rather than reproducing it: NanoAPI's stated differentiator is **"code splitting via the UI"** — automated refactoring toward microservice-friendly boundaries for API codebases specifically — a concrete, judgeable, recurring engineering task (does this monolith get smaller and better-bounded), not "understand your code" in the abstract. That narrowing, by a team that studied CodeSee's failure directly, is itself a data point: even a founder consciously building "CodeSee, but this time it works" chose to anchor the product to a specific recurring job rather than re-attempt the general destination-view thesis.

## 4. Aider's repo-map — the cheapest version of the idea, and what it proves

### 4.1 The algorithm, verified against the actual source

`research-storage.md` §7.4 already establishes Aider's repo-map as the credible *storage* floor for this whole research program ("no persistent storage of any kind... in memory, rebuilt from scratch on every invocation"). This section goes one level deeper, into the *algorithm*, verified by reading `aider/repomap.py` directly (https://github.com/Aider-AI/aider/blob/main/aider/repomap.py) rather than trusting the blog description, because the blog post (https://aider.chat/2023/10/22/repomap.html) undersells exactly how deliberate the ranking mechanics are:

1. **Extraction.** tree-sitter tags queries pull definitions and references per file — the same primitive `research-extraction.md` §2.1 designates AWB's own mandatory Tier 1 (Aider's own docs note this replaced an earlier, GPL-licensed universal-ctags-based version specifically to drop the external-binary dependency and gain richer call-signature output, https://aider.chat/docs/ctags.html — the identical ctags-vs-tree-sitter tradeoff `research-extraction.md` §2.11 independently reaches the same conclusion on).
2. **Graph construction.** A `networkx.MultiDiGraph` where nodes are *files* and a weighted edge is added from the file that *references* an identifier to the file that *defines* it, for every (identifier, referencing-file) pair, with `num_refs` count dampened by `sqrt()` — the source comment states the reason directly: **"scale down so high freq (low value) mentions don't dominate."**
3. **Weighting (the part no secondary source got exactly right).** A multiplier `mul` starts at 1.0 per identifier and is adjusted *before* the reference loop: **×10** if the identifier was explicitly mentioned by the user in chat; **×10** (independently, so both can apply, up to ×100) if the identifier is long (≥8 chars) and snake_case, kebab-case, or camelCase — a cheap, effective proxy for "this is a meaningfully-named symbol, not a throwaway local"; **×0.1** if the identifier is underscore-prefixed (conventionally private); **×0.1** if the identifier has more than 5 distinct definers (i.e., an overloaded/ambiguous/common name, which is otherwise noise). Then, per individual edge, an additional **×50** is applied if the *referencing* file is already open in the active chat — i.e., "what does the code I'm already looking at depend on" dominates the ranking.
4. **Personalized PageRank.** `networkx.pagerank(G, weight="weight", personalization=..., dangling=...)` with a default personalization mass of `100 / num_files` per file, boosted for files already in chat or matching a mentioned path component — this is what lets "show me where the auth flow lives" bias the walk toward auth-adjacent files without a bespoke query language.
5. **Redistribution and ranking.** Each file's PageRank score is redistributed across its own out-edges proportional to edge weight, accumulated per `(defining_file, identifier)` pair, and sorted — turning a file-level graph score into a ranked list of individual *definitions* to show, not just files to include wholesale.
6. **Token-budget enforcement via literal binary search.** `get_ranked_tags_map_uncached` runs a textbook binary search over how many ranked tags to include: `lower_bound=0`, `upper_bound=num_tags`, initial guess `middle = min(max_map_tokens // 25, num_tags)` (a cheap "~25 tokens per tag" heuristic seed), then repeatedly renders the tree for `ranked_tags[:middle]`, counts actual tokens, and narrows the bounds — accepting a result once it is within 15% of the target budget (default 1,024 tokens; a small "no files in chat yet" case is allowed up to 8× that ceiling via `map_mul_no_files=8`). No LLM call is involved anywhere in this pipeline; it is deterministic, and — per `research-storage.md` §7.4 — rebuilt from nothing on every single invocation.

### 4.2 What this validates in `research-extraction.md` / `research-visualization.md`

Every architectural primitive AWB has already committed to appears here in miniature, independently arrived at by a different team solving a narrower problem: **extract-then-aggregate** (file-level graph, not a raw per-call-site edge list — the same collapse `research-extraction.md` §5.3's "aggregate reference edges" recommendation makes for a different reason, storage cost, rather than context-budget cost); **rank, don't dump** (PageRank picking the highest-value subset — structurally the same move as `research-ontology.md` §8.7 and `research-visualization.md` §2.5's "render the aggregated graph, not the raw one"); and **a hard, enforced budget rather than a best-effort truncation** (Aider's binary search is a smaller-scale cousin of the bounded-BFS discipline `research-storage.md` §2.4 mandates for `graph_neighbors`/`graph_blast_radius`, and of the 20–50-node initial-render budget `research-visualization.md` §6.3 recommends for the UI). That three independently-motivated documents in this research program and one completely unrelated, widely-used, revenue-generating open-source tool converge on "extract, aggregate, rank against a hard budget, never dump the raw graph" is strong outside confirmation that this is the right general shape, not an artifact of this research program talking to itself.

### 4.3 What this challenges

The challenge is about *sequencing and default expectations*, not about the shape. Aider's repo-map gets real, admitted-into-production value — every Aider user, on every request, gets a repo map — from an implementation that has **no database, no incremental-update machinery, no confidence scoring, no LLM enrichment, and no visualization UI**: it is Tier 1 alone (`research-extraction.md` §5.1), recomputed fresh every time, at whatever the marginal CPU cost of a single tree-sitter pass plus one PageRank call is. This is direct evidence that AWB should treat Tier 1 as independently shippable and independently valuable — not as a foundation that only pays off once Tiers 1.5/2/3 and the full sigma.js/cosmos.gl visualization stack (`research-visualization.md` §7) are built on top of it. It also implies a concrete sequencing discipline: Aider's own history is ctags → tree-sitter, two iterations of the *cheap* tier, not a single leap to SCIP/LSP-grade precision — the pattern to copy is "improve the free tier" before "add an expensive tier," which maps directly onto pitfall #6 in §6.

## 5. Cursor and Devin — what the best-funded 2026 AI coding tools chose *not* to build

### 5.1 Cursor: embeddings + AST-chunking + a fast grep, deliberately no persistent graph

Cursor's own engineering blog (https://cursor.com/blog/secure-codebase-indexing) and docs (https://cursor.com/docs/context/codebase-indexing) describe a two-part system with no explicit symbol/reference graph anywhere in it. **Chunking** parses each file into an AST via tree-sitter and splits along syntactic boundaries (function, class, method, block) rather than fixed token windows, merging small sibling nodes up to a token limit while keeping a coherent unit (a function body) intact — this is the same "split code by syntax, not by tokens" principle `research-extraction.md`'s tags-query approach already assumes, applied to embedding-chunk boundaries instead of graph-node boundaries. **Storage and freshness** is a Merkle tree: a cryptographic hash per file plus per-folder hashes composed from their children's hashes, so re-sync only has to walk the branches whose hash changed — for a 50,000-file workspace this keeps the always-transferred metadata to roughly 3.2 MB — and a `simhash` fingerprint of that tree lets a new user's client reuse an already-indexed teammate's embeddings from the same org instead of re-embedding from scratch, which Cursor reports cuts median onboarding indexing time from 7.87 seconds to 525 milliseconds (90th percentile: 2.82 minutes → 1.87 seconds; 99th percentile: 4.03 hours → 21 seconds). **Privacy**: filenames are obfuscated and code chunks are encrypted server-side, decrypted client-side only when Agent actually searches, so plaintext source is never persisted on Cursor's servers. **Retrieval** at query time is nearest-neighbor search over the embedding store (Turbopuffer, a vector+full-text hybrid search engine), *plus* a separate, custom-built grep ("Instant Grep") the docs describe as outperforming `ripgrep` on large codebases for exact-pattern and regex queries. Nowhere in any of this is there a persisted table of resolved symbol definitions and call edges that survives across sessions and answers "what calls this function" as a lookup — that capability, to the extent Cursor's agent needs it in a given turn, is reconstructed live from AST-navigation-plus-grep-plus-embeddings, not read from a precomputed graph.

### 5.2 Devin/DeepWiki: the graph is a generated wiki for humans; the agent's real tool is agentic search

Cognition's public architecture is split cleanly into two products that answer two different questions. **DeepWiki** (launched April 25, 2025, https://docs.devin.ai/work-with-devin/deepwiki) automatically indexes a repository — Devin's own docs describe it running on a periodic sweep and producing a hierarchical wiki: architecture-diagram pages, module summaries, and cross-referenced source links, with regeneration cost denominated in "ACUs" (Agent Compute Units) at three effort tiers (Low, free/default; Medium, ~5–10 ACU; High, ~20–40 ACU), and a `.devin/wiki.json` escape hatch for manually steering which pages get generated. A third-party technical case-study write-up (ZenML's LLMOps database, https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/building-an-autonomous-ai-software-engineer-with-advanced-codebase-understanding-and-specialized-model-training-6bnir) describes the underlying representation as files-as-nodes, relationships-as-edges, deliberately enriched with **non-code metadata** — pull-request history, commit history, team discussion — on the premise that "concepts" matter more than raw code structure alone; within weeks of public launch the service had indexed tens of thousands of public repositories (Cognition's own count: over 50,000, https://cognition.com/blog/deepwiki). Critically, this whole apparatus is positioned as **documentation for humans to read** (replace `github.com` with `deepwiki.com` in any repo URL) — it is a generated artifact, not a live query interface the agent calls mid-task. The agent's actual moment-to-moment research tool is a *separate* capability, **Devin Search**, described as going beyond "standard RAG implementations" with "junk removal, advanced filtering, re-ranking, and multi-hop search" (ZenML, ibid.) — i.e., iterative, agentic retrieval over the raw codebase, not a graph traversal against a precomputed schema. The explicit design premise behind this split, per the same source, is that "every large codebase represents its own narrow domain" — favoring a general-purpose search-and-verify loop that adapts per-repository over a fixed, precomputed structure that has to be kept in sync.

### 5.3 The industry's revealed preference, and why AWB's bet is different anyway

Neither of the best-capitalized, fastest-growing AI-native coding tools of 2026 chose "build and maintain a persistent, queryable, explicit symbol/reference graph" as their primary mechanism for codebase understanding. Both independently chose a variant of **agentic search over a freshness-cheap index** (embeddings for Cursor, agentic multi-hop search for Devin) plus, at most, a *generated, human-facing* structural view as a side artifact rather than the agent's own working memory. A synthesis piece surveying this pattern across Cursor, Claude Code, and Devin argues the underlying reason is structural, not merely an engineering-cost shortcut: embeddings are good at semantic similarity ("dog" near "puppy") but "not good at capturing structural relationships in code" — a call to `processPayment()` is not semantically similar to its own definition — while the actual failure mode of retrieval-based tools is silent, not graceful: a document-QA system with a bad retrieval just gives an incomplete answer, but a coding agent with a bad retrieval "write[s] broken code... confidently generate[s] code that calls non-existent methods" (https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/is-rag-dead-what-ai-agents-use-instead). That argument is actually a point *in favor* of AWB's approach relative to pure-embedding retrieval, not against it — it is an argument against embeddings-only, and AWB's design was never embeddings-only (`research-ontology.md` §8.4's `CALLS`/`IMPLEMENTS`/`USES_TYPE` structural edges are exactly the "structural relationship" class embeddings are conceded to miss). The honest reading of §5.1–5.2 is narrower and more useful: two well-resourced competitors judged that *keeping a persistent graph fresh and correct* was not worth its cost relative to cheaper, self-healing alternatives (re-embed on Merkle-tree-detected change; re-search agentically every turn) — which is precisely the cost `research-incremental.md` exists to bound for AWB, not a signal that the graph itself is the wrong idea. AWB's ontology graph is closer in kind to Sourcegraph's precise layer (structural, persistent, exact) than to Cursor's or Devin's approach — which is fine, but it means AWB inherits Sourcegraph's specific adoption risk (§1, §6) rather than getting to claim Cursor's or Devin's growth curve as evidence the approach is validated by the market; it isn't, yet, by either of those two.

## 6. Pitfalls this AWB feature must specifically avoid

Each pitfall below is traced to a named prior-art failure (or, for #2 and #7, a named prior-art *success* whose absence is the failure mode). "Being adopted, not just admired" is defined concretely at the end of each.

**1. Do not ship the ontology graph as a standalone "understand your code" destination view — Sourcetrail's and CodeSee's shared failure mode (§2.4, §3.4).** Both products' core capability worked; both were packaged as a separate place to go, used occasionally, against a free or already-workflow-embedded alternative. Adoption concretely requires: the ontology graph's first-class surfaces are MCP tool calls an agent already makes mid-task (`graph_blast_radius` before a risky edit, `graph_neighbors` while investigating a ticket) and inline affordances inside AWB's existing ticket/chat/review surfaces — never a "Graph" tab whose only purpose is to be opened and browsed.

**2. Anchor the human-facing view to a recurring, already-budgeted job — Sourcegraph's positive lesson, inverted from Sourcetrail/CodeSee's negative one (§1.3, §2.3).** Sourcegraph's paying use cases (security-incident blast radius, compliance audit, cross-repo migration) have a deadline and an owner whose job title includes them; Sourcetrail/CodeSee's use case ("help me understand this codebase") is real but has no recurring budget line, which is why CodeSee's own founder cites "inconsistent" revenue despite real growth. Adoption concretely requires: identify which *existing* AWB workflow (ticket triage, PR review, incident response inside AWB's own ticket lifecycle) the graph measurably speeds up, and instrument that speedup, rather than measuring "graph view opened."

**3. Do not expect an AI/semantic-enrichment layer to rescue a structural product nobody was already using — CodeSee's pivot-then-death (§3.2–3.3).** CodeSee's own CEO stated the AI pivot didn't change "our hypotheses and our mission and our use cases" — it added polish to the same underlying thesis, and the company still closed seven months later, with GenAI explicitly cited as having made the *competitive* landscape harder, not the product's fate easier. Adoption concretely requires: `research-extraction.md` Tier 3 (LLM enrichment) and `research-ontology.md`'s semantic/`curated` layers must never be the argument for why the graph is worth building — the structural Tier 1/1.5 layers must independently justify their existence first (see pitfall #6), with semantic enrichment purely additive.

**4. Respect the maintenance-burden ceiling a small, non-dedicated team can actually sustain — Sourcetrail's concrete collapse mechanism (§2.2).** Separate native indexers per supported language, cross-platform desktop packaging, and two founders whose day jobs and geography diverged is precisely what stopped shipping. AWB is structurally safer here by design — `research-extraction.md` §5.1's tree-sitter-only mandatory tier is one WASM dependency, not several native per-language indexers, and SCIP/LSP are explicitly optional, out-of-process, per `research-extraction.md` §5.1 Tier 2/2b — but the same trap re-opens at the edges: per-language `ast-grep` rule packs (`research-extraction.md` §2.2) and workspace-profile type extensions (`research-ontology.md` §9) must be budgeted additively, language-by-language, against real demand — never promised as "we support your language" up front the way Sourcetrail's multi-indexer commitment implicitly was.

**5. Never let the graph silently go stale — none of the five prior-art subjects solved this, which is exactly why `research-incremental.md`'s staleness discipline is load-bearing, not optional.** Sourcetrail was a point-in-time desktop scan re-run manually; CodeSee's entire differentiated pitch was "self-updating" diagrams, and the company still couldn't retain payers, meaning freshness alone was never the differentiator that mattered; Cursor and Devin sidestep the question by re-deriving from source every few minutes/turns rather than maintaining durable state at all (§5). A graph that looks authoritative but is quietly wrong is worse than "no graph, just grep" — the baseline Cursor and Devin both chose. Adoption concretely requires: the `dirty_ratio`/staleness marker already specified in `research-ontology.md` §8.6 point 6 must be visible at the point of consumption — in the MCP tool response payload an agent reads, not only in a UI corner a human might never look at (echoing `research-extraction.md` §5.3's `{source, confidence, indexed_at, commit}` contract).

**6. Treat every tier above Tier 1 as needing to beat a zero-persistence, zero-LLM baseline before it ships as anything other than optional — Aider's validated existence proof (§4.3).** Aider gets real, shipped, revenue-relevant value from tree-sitter-plus-PageRank-plus-a-token-budget alone, rebuilt from scratch on every call, with no database and no LLM. Adoption concretely requires: before SCIP ingestion, LLM enrichment, or the full sigma.js/cosmos.gl visualization UI (`research-visualization.md` §7) are treated as required-path features, measure whether agents/users get materially better task outcomes from that tier versus Tier-1-only — and follow Aider's own sequencing instinct (ctags → tree-sitter, two iterations of the *cheap* tier before ever reaching for compiler-grade precision) rather than building top-down.

**7. AWB's bet is Sourcegraph's bet, not Cursor's or Devin's — inherit the adoption discipline that implies, not the decade of runway (§5.3).** The two fastest-growing 2026 AI coding products explicitly did not build a persistent symbol graph as their primary mechanism; Sourcegraph did, and it took roughly a decade, a forced self-hosted pivot, and ~$248M to reach durable adoption. AWB does not have that runway for a single feature inside a broader platform. Adoption concretely requires: the ontology graph must prove incremental value over the free grep/agentic-search baseline every AWB agent already has via its existing tools, on a much shorter timeline than Sourcegraph had — or it will be quietly bypassed the way a slower, heavier alternative to an already-free built-in tool always is, the identical "why pay when the free thing already does this" logic (`fxtentacle`, §2.3) that helped kill Sourcetrail against JetBrains' bundled navigation.

**8. Measure re-opens and repeat MCP calls, not first-opens — CodeSee's own numbers are the warning (§3.4).** Doubling product logos and adding thousands of free users in a single year did not save CodeSee; the miss was recurrence, not discovery. A visually striking graph view will *always* get positive first reactions — that is not evidence of adoption. Adoption concretely requires: AWB's own success metric for this feature, from the first release, must be a recurrence measure (MCP tool-call frequency per ticket per agent, human graph-view re-opens per workspace per week, or similar) rather than a first-touch/signup-style metric — the same "small, bounded, expand only on demonstrated demand" discipline `research-visualization.md` §6.3 already applies to node-render budgets, applied here to the feature's own success criteria instead.
